Abstract

Vision–language models (VLMs) show promising performance in surgical visual question answering (VQA). However, existing surgical VQA datasets often contain linguistic shortcuts, where question phrasing implicitly constrains the answer space. In safety-critical surgical settings, it remains unclear whether reported performance reflects visual understanding or reliance on such shortcuts.

We introduce SurgCheck, a diagnostic benchmark for quantifying linguistic shortcut reliance. Each surgical frame is paired with an original question containing entity names and a less-biased counterpart that removes these names while preserving identical visual content and ground-truth answers. The resulting performance gap provides a diagnostic signal of shortcut reliance. To keep less-biased questions well-defined, four grounding cues are incorporated: bounding box, arrow, spatial position, and periphrasis.

Across five VLMs we observe consistent degradation on less-biased questions despite identical visual inputs. A text-only ablation reveals minimal drops for action and target prediction — indicating these are largely driven by linguistic shortcuts rather than visual reasoning. SurgCheck shows that strong benchmark performance does not necessarily imply faithful visual understanding.

How SurgCheck Works

Built on scene-graph annotations from Endoscapes-SG201, each frame produces an original and a less-biased question that differ only in linguistic bias. A VLM answers, and an LLM-as-a-Judge (GPT-5-mini) scores semantic correctness.

Conclusion

Surgical VQA is inherently prone to strong linguistic bias. Question phrasing — instrument names, hand references, and conventional instrument–action–target patterns — is highly correlated with the answer, so models can, and do, exploit textual shortcuts instead of grounding their predictions in the image.

SurgCheck makes this reliance measurable. The paired-question gaps and the text-only ablation show it is systematic: performance stays high on original questions even when the image is removed, especially for action- and target-centric tasks. In other words, strong benchmark numbers do not guarantee faithful visual understanding.

This has a direct implication for how surgical VQA models should be built. Training and evaluation should:

  • Verify visual grounding — explicitly check whether a model truly uses visual evidence (e.g. via bias-controlled paired questions and text-only diagnostics) rather than trusting aggregate accuracy.
  • Prevent shortcut learning — adopt strategies such as bias-controlled data, less-biased phrasing, and grounding supervision so that models are forced to look at the image to answer.

We hope SurgCheck supports more reliable evaluation of multimodal models and encourages methods that genuinely ground predictions in visual evidence.
